> research: UK small-business payment culture remains a chronic challenge, with cash-flow pressures and late payments often driven by customer delays and administrative friction. The Government’s 2024 late-payments research found that the most common payment terms are 30 days (54%), but many micro businesses report late payments from customers (32% cite this as a cause of late payments to them) and a notable minority (18%) believe some customers delay payments deliberately as a form of “free finance.” Administrative errors were also a frequent cause (24%), and micro businesses were less likely to pursue late payments formally (about 19%) than larger firms (around 42%), largely to protect relationships. The research also shows that 17% of invoices are late on average across sectors. These findings underpin a staged, relationship-aware chase approach that starts with a gentle nudge and progresses toward firmer action as needed [1]. In addition, independent small-business surveys in 2025 show a sizable share of businesses are owed money (about 62%), with average unpaid invoices around £21,400, and a sizable portion of invoices overdue by 30+ days (about 54%). This evidence reinforces the value of timely reminders and clear payment expectations to protect cash flow in busy hospitality settings [2]. Furthermore, the government’s 2026 Small Business Protections Bill intends to cap large-firm payment terms at 60 days and introduce statutory interest on late payments (8% above the Bank of England base rate), with the Small Business Commissioner empowered to enforce compliance—an important contextual backdrop for practitioners chasing late payments in the UK hospitality sector [3]. The statutory interest framework remains 8% above BoE base rate; current base-rate-linked figures place the rate around 11.75% per year, depending on the BoE base rate at the time [4]. In practice, reputable templates for early-stage Chaser/FreeAgent-style reminders advocate a warm-to-firm progression, and many UK practitioners begin with a non-confrontational reminder before escalating to formal notices if needed [5][6]. GOV.UK’s late-payments research highlights that cash-flow constraints and late payments from customers are common, with administrative errors and deliberate late payments also playing a role; micro businesses are less likely to pursue formal remedies, often to preserve relationships, reinforcing a gentle-to-firm sequencing for chasing payments [1]. The 2025 QuickBooks UK report reinforces the scale of the problem—62% of UK small businesses are owed money, with average unpaid invoices around £21.4k and more than half of invoices overdue by 30+ days—underlining the financial stakes of timely collection and the rationale for a clear but polite escalation when responses stall [2].
